628 · Number theory

Pell's equation and the chakravala method

Integer solutions of equations of the form N x^2 + 1 = y^2 were found by composition and cyclic methods, including the case 61 with a very large minimal solution.

Historical record

These milestones show distinct stages of the idea. Dates and priority claims can be uncertain; consult the evidence and notes for each stage.

  1. 400 BCE

    Side and diagonal numbers: approximations to the square root of 2 in Greece and India

    precursor

    Pythagorean side/diagonal numbers; Baudhayana approximations 17/12, 577/408

    Solutions of the N = 2 case of the equation give successive rational approximations of the square root of 2, known by about 400 BC.

    Evidence (1)
    • Wikipedia Pell equation: as early as 400 BC in India and Greece mathematicians studied the N=2 case Wikipedia, Pell equation
  2. 250

    Diophantus considers equations equivalent to Pell-type problems

    precursor

    Diophantus, Arithmetica

    Diophantus solves problems of the form a x^2 + c = y^2 for several small cases, equivalent to Pell-type equations.

    Evidence (2)
    • Wikipedia Pell equation: around AD 250 Diophantus considered equivalent equation for (a,c) = (1,1), (1,-1), (1,12), (3,9) Wikipedia, Pell equation
    • MacTutor Pell: Diophantus examines problems related to Pell equation MacTutor, Pell equation
  3. 628

    Brahmagupta composition (bhavana/samasa): generating solutions of x^2 - N y^2 = k

    first statement

    Brahmagupta, Brahmasphutasiddhanta, chapter 18, 628

    Brahmagupta proves an identity composing two solutions into a third, so that one solution yields infinitely many, and solves cases such as N = 92 (1151, 120).

    Evidence (3)
    • MacTutor Pell: Brahmagupta lemma discovered in 628, method of composition, first to study the equation MacTutor, Pell equation
    • Wikipedia Pell equation: Brahmagupta identity, composes triples, solved N = 92 with (1151, 120) Wikipedia, Pell equation
    • Colebrooke 1817 translation of Brahmagupta and Bhaskara (Algebra, with Arithmetic and Mensuration); Open Library Open Library
  4. 950–1000

    Jayadeva: general cyclic method

    key development

    Jayadeva, c. 950-1000 (quoted in later works); Selenius analysis 1975

    Jayadeva shows how to generalise Brahmagupta approach into the cyclic (chakravala) method.

    Evidence (2)
    • Wikipedia Chakravala method: Jayadeva (c. 950-1000) pointed out that Brahmagupta approach could be generalised Wikipedia, Chakravala method
    • Selenius 1975, Rationale of the chakravala process of Jayadeva and Bhaskara II Historia Mathematica 2 (1975)
  5. 1150

    Bhaskara II: chakravala solves N = 61 (x = 226153980, y = 1766319049)

    key development

    Bhaskara II, Bijaganita, 1150

    Bhaskara II gives the cyclic algorithm that always terminates in a solution, and applies it to N = 61 where the smallest solution is huge.

    Evidence (3)
    • MacTutor Pell: Bhaskara II in 1150 discovered the cyclic method (chakravala) MacTutor, Pell equation
    • MacTutor Bhaskara II: studied p = 8, 11, 32, 61, 67; gives x = 226153980 for 61 MacTutor, Bhaskara II
    • Wikipedia Pell equation: first general method for all N given by Bhaskara II in 1150; used for N = 61 Wikipedia, Pell equation
  6. 1657–1658

    Fermat's challenge; Brouncker and Wallis solve it (Commercium epistolicum 1658)

    key development

    Fermat 1657 challenge; Wallis, Commercium epistolicum, 1658

    Fermat challenges English mathematicians, and Brouncker finds a method close to continued fractions; Wallis publishes the exchange in 1658.

    Evidence (2)
    • MacTutor Pell: European interest began with Fermat challenge of 1657; Wallis published exchange in 1658 MacTutor, Pell equation
    • Open Library lists Commercium epistolicum (Brouncker and Wallis), 1658 Open Library
  7. 1766–1769

    Lagrange proves infinitely many solutions and systematises the continued-fraction method

    consolidation

    Lagrange, work of 1766-1769; Additions to Euler Elements of Algebra, 1771

    Lagrange gives a rigorous proof that the equation always has infinitely many solutions via continued fractions.

    Evidence (2)
    • MacTutor Pell: Euler continued-fraction approach polished by Lagrange in 1766; Additions 1771 proves infinitely many solutions MacTutor, Pell equation
    • Wikipedia Pell equation: general theory developed by Lagrange in 1766-1769 Wikipedia, Pell equation
  8. Date unknown

    Euler names it after Pell (misattribution)

    naming

    Euler, naming of the equation

    Euler, confusing Brouncker with Pell, gave the equation the name it still carries in the West.

    Evidence (2)
    • MacTutor Pell: Euler named it Pell equation, probably by confusing Brouncker and Pell MacTutor, Pell equation
    • Wikipedia Pell equation: Pell revised the translation of Rahn Teutsche Algebra (1659); Euler named it after him by mistake Wikipedia, Pell equation
