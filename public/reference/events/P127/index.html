Ptolemy's Almagest

Ptolemy extended chord tables with sum, difference and half-angle formulas for astronomical computation.

Locate this event on the map ↗

Historical record

These milestones show distinct stages of the idea. Dates and priority claims can be uncertain; consult the evidence and notes for each stage.

  1. 140 BCE

    Hipparchus' chord tables and solar and lunar models

    precursor

    Hipparchus supplied the chord table, the solar eccentric model and a lunar epicycle model that the Almagest builds on.

    Evidence (2)
    • The Syntaxis adopted Hipparchus' solar model; he made models for Sun and Moon but not the planets. Wikipedia, Almagest
    • Most of what is known about Hipparchus comes from the Almagest. MacTutor, Hipparchus
  2. 147–148

    Canopic Inscription

    precursor

    Ptolemy, inscription at Canopus, 147 or 148 AD

    A public inscription gives an earlier version of Ptolemy's models, so the Almagest is later than it.

    Evidence (1)
    • Hamilton found that the models in the inscription are earlier than those in the Almagest, so the Almagest dates from about 150 or after. Wikipedia, Almagest
  3. 150

    The Almagest (Mathematike Syntaxis)

    first statement

    Ptolemy, Mathematike Syntaxis, c. 150 AD

    Ptolemy's thirteen books give the mathematical theory of the Sun, Moon and planets, with chord trigonometry in Book I.

    Evidence (3)
    • Written possibly around 150, in thirteen books; Book I has the chord table. Wikipedia, Almagest
    • It is a treatise in thirteen books; the first two present the trigonometric tools. MacTutor, Ptolemy
    • Standard English translation. Toomer, Ptolemy's Almagest (Springer, 1984)
  4. 150

    Chord trigonometry applied to the planets

    first application

    Ptolemy, Almagest, Books II-XIII

    Ptolemy uses the chord function and spherical methods throughout to compute the motions of Sun, Moon and planets, and adds the equant for the planets.

    Evidence (2)
    • He introduces the equant and succeeds in modelling the planets where Hipparchus did not. Wikipedia, Almagest
    • He first sets out the mathematics needed, then applies it to the Sun and Moon. MacTutor, Ptolemy
  5. 800–899

    Arabic translations

    key development

    Arabic Almagest (9th century)

    Arabic scholars began translating the work in the ninth century; the oldest surviving copies are from then.

    Evidence (1)
    • The text survives in copies; Arabic scholars began to translate it in the 9th century. Wikipedia, Almagest
  6. 1175

    Almagestum: the name from Arabic al-majisti

    naming

    Gerard of Cremona, Latin translation from Arabic (finished 1175)

    The name Almagest comes from the Arabic form of the Greek superlative and was spread by Gerard's Latin version.

    Evidence (2)
    • The name was popularised by the Latin Almagestum of the 12th century; Gerard finished in 1175. Wikipedia, Almagest
    • MacTutor explains the chain from Mathematike Syntaxis to Almagest. MacTutor, Ptolemy
  7. 1496

    Regiomontanus' Epitome of the Almagest printed

    consolidation

    Regiomontanus, Epytoma in Almagestum Ptolemaei, printed 1496 (completed in manuscript 1462)

    Regiomontanus condensed the Almagest into a guide that was printed in 1496; it was completed in manuscript in 1462.

    Evidence (3)
    • He completed the Epitome in 1462. MacTutor, Regiomontanus
    • Open Library dates the printed Epytoma to 1496. Regiomontanus, Epytoma in Almagestum Ptolemaei
    • Open Library lists Epytoma in Almagestum Ptolemaei with first publication year 1496 Open Library search
  8. 1984

    Toomer's English translation

    key development

    G. J. Toomer, Ptolemy's Almagest (Springer, 1984)

    Toomer's annotated translation from the Greek became the standard modern text.

    Evidence (2)
    • Springer 1984 listed in zbMATH; Wikipedia names it as the second English translation. Wikipedia, Almagest
    • Open Library record of the work. Toomer, Ptolemy's Almagest
