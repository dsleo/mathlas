1657 · Probability

Probability from games of chance

The problem of points and dice questions are solved by counting equally likely cases, then generalised into a calculus of chance and, with Laplace, an analytic one.

Historical record

These milestones show distinct stages of the idea. Dates and priority claims can be uncertain; consult the evidence and notes for each stage.

  1. 1494

    Pacioli's proportional division of stakes

    precursor

    Luca Pacioli, Summa de arithmetica, geometrica, proportioni et proportionalita, 1494

    Early printed treatment of the interrupted game that divided the stake by rounds already won, a rule Tartaglia later criticised; neither used counting of future outcomes.

    Evidence (1)
    • Pacioli treated the interrupted game in 1494 by proportion to rounds won; Tartaglia found the rule defective. Wikipedia: Problem of points
  2. 1560

    Cardano's dice analysis

    precursor

    Girolamo Cardano, Liber de ludo aleae, written 1560s, printed 1663

    Cardano counted outcomes of dice throws in the 1560s, but the text circulated only in print about a century later.

    Evidence (1)
    • Cardano's dice work dates from the 1560s and was not published until around 100 years later. Wikipedia: History of probability
  3. 1654

    Pascal-Fermat correspondence solves the problem of points by counting equally likely futures

    precursor

    Pascal-Fermat letters, summer 1654

    In five letters the two settled the division of stakes by counting the equally likely ways the game could have continued, introducing what is effectively expectation.

    Evidence (2)
    • Their correspondence of summer 1654 treated the dice problem and the problem of points. MacTutor: Blaise Pascal
    • Fermat's method counted the equally likely continuations; Pascal gave a step-by-step recursion and a binomial formula. Wikipedia: Problem of points
  4. 1657

    Huygens' printed treatise on reasoning in games of chance

    first statement

    Christiaan Huygens, De ratiociniis in ludo aleae, in F. van Schooten, Exercitationum mathematicarum, 1657

    The first published treatise devoted to probability, solving gambling problems by an expectation principle.

    Evidence (3)
    • Archive.org record dates De ratiociniis in ludo aleae to 1657. archive.org item DeRatiociniisInLudoAleae
    • Huygens' 1657 text is described as the first published book wholly on probability. Wikipedia: History of probability
    • MacTutor reproduces the first page of De ratiociniis in ludo aleae (1657). MacTutor: Christiaan Huygens
  5. 1713

    Ars conjectandi and the law of large numbers

    key development

    Jakob Bernoulli, Ars conjectandi, Basel, 1713 (posthumous)

    Bernoulli's posthumous book proved the first version of the law of large numbers and recast probability as a general art of conjecture.

    Evidence (2)
    • Archive.org records list the 1713 Basel edition. archive.org item jacobibernoulli00bern
    • Ars Conjectandi (1713) introduced the law of large numbers. Wikipedia: History of probability
  6. 1718

    De Moivre's Doctrine of Chances

    consolidation

    Abraham de Moivre, The Doctrine of Chances, London, 1718

    An English textbook-style treatment extending chance calculations to games, mortality and annuities.

    Evidence (2)
    • Archive.org record dates the first edition to 1718. archive.org item bim_eighteenth-century_the-doctrine-of-chances_moivre-abraham-de_1718
    • De Moivre's 1718 book extended probability to more complex problems. Wikipedia: History of probability
  7. 1812

    Laplace's analytic theory of probabilities

    key development

    Pierre-Simon Laplace, Theorie analytique des probabilites, Paris, 1812

    Laplace brought analytic methods (generating functions, asymptotics) to the subject.

    Evidence (2)
    • Archive.org record dates the Paris edition to 1812. archive.org item theorieanalytiqu0000mlec
    • Laplace built on earlier work in his Theorie analytique des probabilites. Wikipedia: History of probability
