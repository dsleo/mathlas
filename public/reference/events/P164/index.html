300 BCE · Number theory

Euclid's Elements VII–IX: deductive arithmetic

Divisibility, the Euclidean algorithm and geometric progressions of integers were developed as a self-contained deductive body.

Locate this event on the map ↗

Historical record

These milestones show distinct stages of the idea. Dates and priority claims can be uncertain; consult the evidence and notes for each stage.

  1. 450 BCE–350 BCE

    Pythagorean odd and even arithmetic

    precursor

    Fifth-century Pythagoreans handled odd and even numbers; van der Waerden suggests Book VII draws on a Pythagorean textbook, and the Euclidean algorithm may predate Euclid.

    Evidence (2)
    • Numbers and the odd and the even play a prominent role in Philolaus (Frs. 4-5). Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Pythagoreanism
    • Van der Waerden suggests Book VII derives from a Pythagorean number-theory textbook; the algorithm was probably known to Eudoxus around 375 BC. Wikipedia, Euclidean algorithm
  2. 400 BCE–350 BCE

    Archytas proves a ratio theorem for numbers

    precursor

    Archytas, as reported by Boethius and used in the Sectio canonis

    Archytas showed that no number lies in proportion between n and n+1, an early proof about integers.

    Evidence (1)
    • He proved ratios (n+1):n cannot be divided by a mean proportional. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Archytas
  3. 300 BCE

    Euclid, Elements VII-IX

    first statement

    Euclid, Elements, Books VII-IX, c. 300 BC

    Euclid presents divisibility, the Euclidean algorithm, primes, geometric progressions of integers, infinitely many primes (IX.20) and even perfect numbers (IX.36) as a deductive body.

    Evidence (4)
    • Books 7-9 deal with number theory; Book 7 is a self-contained introduction containing the Euclidean algorithm. MacTutor, Euclid
    • Book IX contains the infinitude of primes, the geometric-series sum and the perfect-number construction. Wikipedia, Euclid's Elements
    • Primary text of Book VII (definitions and the algorithm). D. E. Joyce, Elements Book VII
    • Interpretive study of the arithmetic books. Knorr, Problems in the interpretation of Greek number theory, Stud. Hist. Philos. Sci. 7 (1976)
  4. 300 BCE

    Euclid's Elements as the first deductive arithmetic

    consolidation

    Euclid, Elements, Books VII-IX

    The same books are also the first surviving systematic treatment, so they serve as the field's founding textbook.

    Evidence (1)
    • The Elements is the oldest extant large-scale deductive treatment of mathematics. Wikipedia, Euclid's Elements
  5. 100

    Nicomachus' stand-alone arithmetic

    consolidation

    Nicomachus, Introduction to Arithmetic, c. 100 AD

    Nicomachus writes the first book treating arithmetic apart from geometry, without proofs.

    Evidence (2)
    • It was the first work to treat arithmetic as a separate topic from geometry. MacTutor, Nicomachus
    • Open Library record of the translation. Nicomachus, Introduction to Arithmetic (D'Ooge, 1926)
  6. 1482

    First printed Elements

    key development

    Euclid, Elements, ed. Erhard Ratdolt (Venice, 1482)

    The first printed edition spread Euclid's arithmetic books through Europe.

    Evidence (1)
    • The first printed edition was published by Erhard Ratdolt in 1482. Wikipedia, Euclid's Elements
  7. 1701–1800

    Euler proves the converse of Euclid's perfect-number rule

    key development

    Euler, work on even perfect numbers (18th century)

    Euler showed that every even perfect number has Euclid's form, completing IX.36.

    Evidence (1)
    • Euler proved in the 18th century that the formula yields all even perfect numbers; Alhazen had conjectured it earlier. Wikipedia, Euclid-Euler theorem
