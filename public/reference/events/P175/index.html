300 BCE · Combinatorics

Pingala: combinatorics of poetic metres

Counting patterns of long and short syllables yielded binomial-type identities and a binary-like device; Halayudha later described the triangle.

Locate this event on the map ↗

Historical record

These milestones show distinct stages of the idea. Dates and priority claims can be uncertain; consult the evidence and notes for each stage.

  1. 300 BCE–100 BCE

    Pingala's Chandahshastra

    first statement

    Pingala, Chandahshastra (Chandas Sutra), c. 3rd-2nd c. BCE

    Pingala gives rules to list, index and count all metres built from short and long syllables, which amounts to enumerating binary sequences and binomial counts.

    Evidence (3)
    • The text gives prastara, nashta, uddishta and related procedures and a triangular array of binomial coefficients. Wikipedia, Pingala
    • Dated to the final centuries BCE; the exact date is uncertain. Wikipedia, Pingala
    • Modern study of Indian metrical combinatorics and the Fibonacci link. Singh, The so-called Fibonacci numbers in ancient and medieval India, Historia Math. 12 (1985)
  2. 600–800

    Virahanka's Fibonacci-type counts

    key development

    Virahanka, work on prosody (6th-8th c. AD)

    Virahanka counts metres of a given total length where long syllables count two beats, giving the Fibonacci recurrence.

    Evidence (2)
    • He was an Indian prosodist, 6th to 8th century, building on Pingala, regarded as first to propose the Fibonacci sequence. Wikipedia, Virahanka
    • Singh's survey traces Virahanka, Gopala and Hemachandra. Singh, Historia Math. 12 (1985)
  3. 950–1000

    Halayudha's staircase of Mount Meru

    key development

    Halayudha, Mritasanjivani (commentary on Chandahshastra), 10th c.

    Halayudha explains Pingala's rules with the meru-prastara, a triangular array equivalent to Pascal's triangle, and also names it.

    Evidence (3)
    • His method of pyramidal expansion (meru-prastara) is equivalent to Pascal's triangle; he lived in the 10th century. Wikipedia, Halayudha
    • Same attribution in the history of the triangle. Wikipedia, Pascal's triangle
    • Jaina commentary of the 10th century also shows the triangle. MacTutor, Jaina mathematics
  4. 1665

    Pascal's Treatise on the Arithmetical Triangle

    consolidation

    Pascal, Traite du triangle arithmetique (written 1654, published 1665)

    Pascal collected the properties of the triangle and used them for probability, giving the first systematic European treatment.

    Evidence (2)
    • Published posthumously in 1665; Pascal collected known results and applied them to probability. Wikipedia, Pascal's triangle
    • Open Library record. Pascal, Traite du triangle arithmetique
  5. 1708

    The triangle is named after Pascal

    naming

    Montmort, Essay d'analyse sur les jeux de hazard (1708)

    Montmort called it Pascal's table for combinations, followed by de Moivre in 1730, giving the Western name.

    Evidence (1)
    • Named for Pascal by Montmort (1708) and de Moivre (1730). Wikipedia, Pascal's triangle
  6. 1863

    First Western edition of the Chandahshastra

    key development

    A. Weber, Indische Studien 8 (Leipzig, 1863)

    Weber's edition brought Pingala's text to Western scholars.

    Dating or attribution note

    Volume number comes from Wikipedia; not verified in a library record.

    Evidence (1)
    • Weber's edition is the earliest listed in Wikipedia's editions of the text. Wikipedia, Pingala
