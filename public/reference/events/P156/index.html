450 BCE · Geometry

Discovery of incommensurable magnitudes (irrationality of the diagonal)

The failure of the hypotenuse of a right isosceles triangle to be a ratio of integers broke the identification of magnitude with number and forced a new foundation for geometry.

Locate this event on the map ↗

Historical record

These milestones show distinct stages of the idea. Dates and priority claims can be uncertain; consult the evidence and notes for each stage.

  1. 450 BCE–400 BCE

    First proof that side and diagonal of a square are incommensurable (attributed, disputed)

    first statement

    Pythagorean school; attributed to Hippasus of Metapontum; no surviving text

    A Pythagorean showed that the diagonal of a square has no common measure with its side, breaking the claim that all magnitudes are ratios of whole numbers.

    Evidence (3)
    • Wikipedia Hippasus: sometimes credited with discovery of irrationals; no ancient writer ascribes it to him specifically; he lived c. 530 - c. 450 BC Wikipedia, Hippasus
    • Von Fritz 1945 studies the attribution to Hippasus in the Annals of Mathematics K. von Fritz, Annals of Mathematics 46 (1945)
    • Wikipedia Irrational number: first proof attributed to a Pythagorean, possibly Hippasus, 5th century BC Wikipedia, Irrational number
  2. 400 BCE

    Theodorus proves irrationality of the roots of 3, 5, ... up to 17

    key development

    Reported in Plato, Theaetetus (dialogue set in 399 BC)

    Theodorus handles non-square numbers case by case, without a general theorem, which prompts the general question taken up by Theaetetus.

    Evidence (2)
    • MacTutor Theodorus: Plato describes his proofs for roots of 3, 5, ... 17 and notes that the root of 2 was already known irrational MacTutor, Theodorus
    • MacTutor Theaetetus: the conversation in the dialogue took place in 399 BC MacTutor, Theaetetus
  3. 400 BCE–369 BCE

    Theaetetus develops a general theory of irrational lengths

    key development

    Reconstructed from Euclid, Elements Book X and XIII

    Theaetetus classifies incommensurable lengths in general; later Book X of the Elements is believed to describe his work.

    Evidence (1)
    • MacTutor Theaetetus: Books X and XIII of the Elements are almost certainly a description of his work; born about 417 BC, died about 369 BC MacTutor, Theaetetus
  4. 370 BCE–355 BCE

    Eudoxus defines equality of ratios, covering irrational magnitudes

    key development

    Eudoxus; preserved in Euclid, Elements Book V

    Eudoxus gives a definition of proportion that treats commensurable and incommensurable magnitudes alike, restoring a foundation for geometry.

    Evidence (2)
    • MacTutor Eudoxus: theory of proportion allowing possibly irrational lengths to be compared; lived 408-355 BC MacTutor, Eudoxus
    • Wikipedia Irrational number: Eudoxus formalised a theory of proportion for commensurable and incommensurable quantities Wikipedia, Irrational number
  5. 300 BCE

    Euclid Elements Book V (proportion) and Book X (irrationals)

    consolidation

    Euclid, Elements, Alexandria, c. 300 BC

    Euclid sets out the theory of proportion and a classification of irrational lines as parts of one deductive treatise.

    Evidence (2)
    • MacTutor Euclid: born about 325 BC; the Elements is his treatise; MacTutor Theaetetus says Book X describes irrational lengths MacTutor, Euclid / Theaetetus
    • Fowler 1992 invites reading of Book X of the Elements D. Fowler, Historia Mathematica 19 (1992)
