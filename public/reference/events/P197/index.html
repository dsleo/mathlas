450 BCE · Number theory

Pythagorean number theory and musical ratios

Whole-number ratios behind consonant string lengths supported a programme of studying even, odd, triangular and perfect numbers as objects in themselves.

Locate this event on the map ↗

Historical record

These milestones show distinct stages of the idea. Dates and priority claims can be uncertain; consult the evidence and notes for each stage.

  1. 530 BCE–500 BCE

    Tradition credits Pythagoras with the concord ratios

    precursor

    Later tradition makes Pythagoras the discoverer that octave, fifth and fourth match 2:1, 3:2 and 4:3, though the evidence is indirect.

    Evidence (3)
    • MacTutor says Pythagoras noticed that string lengths in whole-number ratios give harmonious tones. MacTutor, Pythagoras
    • The correspondence is reflected in early Pythagorean sayings and so was probably known to Pythagoras, but the famous workshop story would not work. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Pythagoreanism
    • The same entry doubts that Pythagoras himself was a mathematician or worked out the detailed mathematics. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Pythagoras
  2. 450 BCE–400 BCE

    Philolaus builds the cosmos on the concord ratios

    first statement

    Philolaus, fragments (esp. Fr. 6a), late 5th c. BC

    The earliest surviving Pythagorean text to use the octave, fifth and fourth ratios as a structural principle is Philolaus' book, known only from fragments.

    Evidence (3)
    • Philolaus' conception of the cosmos centred on the match between the three concords and the ratios 2:1, 3:2, 4:3. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Pythagoreanism (Fr. 6a)
    • Philolaus, born about 470, taught the preceding generation of Pythagoreans before Archytas. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Archytas
    • Standard modern study and edition of the fragments. Huffman, Philolaus of Croton (Cambridge University Press, 1993)
  3. 400 BCE–350 BCE

    Archytas: ratio theory and the four sciences

    key development

    Archytas, Fr. 1 and testimonia (DK 47)

    Archytas proved that no mean proportional lies between the numbers n and n+1, a number-theoretic fact behind musical ratios, and named arithmetic, geometry, astronomy and music as sister sciences.

    Evidence (2)
    • He proved that superparticular ratios (n+1):n cannot be split by a mean proportional, and Fr. 1 is the earliest text naming the four sciences. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Archytas
    • Standard modern edition and commentary. Huffman, Archytas of Tarentum (Cambridge University Press, 2005)
  4. 300 BCE

    Euclid proves the perfect-number rule

    key development

    Euclid, Elements, Books VII-IX (IX.36), c. 300 BC

    Euclid defines perfect numbers in Book VII and proves in IX.36 that 2^(k-1)(2^k-1) is perfect when 2^k-1 is prime, turning Pythagorean number lore into a theorem.

    Evidence (3)
    • The first known result on perfect numbers is Elements IX.36. MacTutor, Perfect numbers
    • Definition VII.22 names perfect numbers and IX.36 gives the formation rule. Wikipedia, Perfect number
    • Primary text of IX.36 in translation. D. E. Joyce, Euclid's Elements IX.36
  5. 100

    Nicomachus' Introduction to Arithmetic

    consolidation

    Nicomachus of Gerasa, Introduction to Arithmetic, c. 100 AD

    Nicomachus gives the first stand-alone Pythagorean arithmetic: classes of numbers (perfect, abundant, deficient), figurate numbers and the link to musical ratios, stated without proofs.

    Evidence (3)
    • It was the first work to treat arithmetic apart from geometry and stayed the standard text for centuries. MacTutor, Nicomachus
    • Nicomachus classified numbers by perfect, superabundant and deficient, writing around 100 AD. MacTutor, Perfect numbers
    • English translation by D'Ooge (1926). Nicomachus, Introduction to Arithmetic (Open Library record)
  6. Date unknown

    Mesopotamian tuning and ratio practice

    precursor

    Wikipedia reports that the fifth-based tuning later called Pythagorean already existed in ancient Mesopotamia, so musical ratios were probably used before the Greek theory.

    Evidence (1)
    • Pythagorean tuning is said to date back to ancient Mesopotamia and was misattributed to Pythagoras. Wikipedia, Pythagorean tuning
