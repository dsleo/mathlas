370 BCE · Analysis

Eudoxus: theory of proportion and method of exhaustion

Eudoxus gave a theory of ratios for incommensurables and the exhaustion method, a precursor of integration.

Locate this event on the map ↗

Historical record

These milestones show distinct stages of the idea. Dates and priority claims can be uncertain; consult the evidence and notes for each stage.

  1. 450 BCE–400 BCE

    Discovery of incommensurable magnitudes

    precursor

    The Pythagoreans are credited with finding irrational ratios, which made a new theory of ratio necessary.

    Evidence (2)
    • The discovery of irrationals is attributed to the Pythagoreans, though probably not to Pythagoras. MacTutor, Pythagoras
    • Standard study of the Elements' theory of incommensurables. Knorr, The Evolution of the Euclidean Elements (1975)
  2. 430 BCE

    Antiphon's inscribed polygons

    precursor

    Antiphon doubled the sides of a polygon inscribed in a circle, the first recorded step toward exhausting a curved area.

    Evidence (2)
    • Antiphon inscribed a square, then 8-gon, 16-gon and so on; his methods contained ideas leading to integration. MacTutor, Squaring the circle
    • The exhaustion idea began with Antiphon in the late 5th century BC. Wikipedia, Method of exhaustion
  3. 370 BCE–355 BCE

    Eudoxus' definition of equal ratios

    first statement

    Eudoxus' lost theory, preserved as Euclid, Elements V

    Eudoxus compared ratios of any magnitudes, rational or not, by comparing multiples, which handled incommensurables.

    Evidence (2)
    • He made a definition allowing possibly irrational lengths to be compared; his works are lost. MacTutor, Eudoxus
    • Eudoxus was probably the source for most of Euclid's Book V. Wikipedia, Eudoxus of Cnidus
  4. 370 BCE–355 BCE

    Eudoxus proves the cone and pyramid volumes by exhaustion

    first application

    Eudoxus' proofs, as reported by Archimedes

    Democritus had stated that cone and pyramid are one third of cylinder and prism; Eudoxus supplied the first proof using exhaustion.

    Evidence (2)
    • Heath's introduction says Eudoxus first proved these results scientifically, while Democritus first asserted them. Heath, The Method of Archimedes (1912), introduction
    • Eudoxus made Antiphon's idea rigorous, proving the one-third results later credited to him by Archimedes. MacTutor, Eudoxus
  5. 300 BCE

    Euclid's Elements V and XII

    consolidation

    Euclid, Elements, Books V and XII

    Euclid gathers Eudoxus' theory of proportion in Book V and uses exhaustion to prove area and volume results in Book XII.

    Evidence (4)
    • Proclus says Euclid collected many of Eudoxus' theorems; Book V is probably Eudoxan. Wikipedia, Euclid's Elements
    • Euclid uses exhaustion for six propositions in Book XII. Wikipedia, Method of exhaustion
    • Primary text of the definition of equal ratios. D. E. Joyce, Elements V, definition 5
    • Primary text of XII.2, circles are as squares on diameters. D. E. Joyce, Elements XII.2
  6. 250 BCE–212 BCE

    Archimedes applies exhaustion widely

    key development

    Archimedes, Quadrature of the Parabola, Measurement of a Circle, On the Sphere and Cylinder

    Archimedes states the lemma now named after him and uses exhaustion for the parabola, circle, sphere and spiral.

    Evidence (2)
    • He used exhaustion to obtain a wide range of results. MacTutor, Archimedes
    • Quadrature of the Parabola states the lemma used by Eudoxus and earlier mathematicians. Wikipedia, Archimedes
  7. 1647

    The name 'method of exhaustion'

    naming

    Gregorius a Sancto Vincentio, Opus geometricum quadraturae circuli et sectionum coni (Antwerp, 1647)

    The term exhaustion for the method is credited to Saint-Vincent, who wrote that a quantity is exhausted.

    Dating or attribution note

    Priority for the exact coinage rests on Wikipedia; MacTutor confirms only the book and the idea.

    Evidence (3)
    • The first use of the term was in 1647 by Gregory of Saint Vincent. Wikipedia, Method of exhaustion
    • MacTutor quotes his phrase about a quantity being exhausted and dates the Opus geometricum to 1647. MacTutor, Saint-Vincent
    • Scanned copy of the 1647 book. Internet Archive record
  8. 1872

    Dedekind's real numbers and Eudoxus' ratios

    key development

    Dedekind, Stetigkeit und irrationale Zahlen (1872)

    Dedekind's cuts are often compared with Eudoxus' definition, though whether Eudoxus influenced him is disputed.

    Evidence (4)
    • Eudoxus' work inspired Dedekind's work on real numbers. Wikipedia, Eudoxus of Cnidus
    • MacTutor reports a view that Dedekind's definition was radically original and not influenced by Eudoxus. MacTutor, Eudoxus
    • Stein's comparison of the two theories. Stein, Eudoxos and Dedekind, Synthese 84 (1990)
    • Open Library record of the 1872 booklet. Dedekind, Stetigkeit und irrationale Zahlen
