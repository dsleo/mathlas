147 BCE · Astronomy & trigonometry

Babylonian astronomical parameters enter Greek astronomy

Greek geometric lunar models were tested and calibrated against Babylonian period parameters and eclipse records.

Locate this event on the map ↗

Historical record

These milestones show distinct stages of the idea. Dates and priority claims can be uncertain; consult the evidence and notes for each stage.

  1. 432 BCE

    Meton's solstice and 19-year cycle

    precursor

    Meton's observation and cycle may reflect Babylonian period relations and were later used by Hipparchus and Ptolemy.

    Evidence (2)
    • Meton's observations proved very useful to later astronomers; it is unclear if the 19-year cycle was independent of Mesopotamia. MacTutor, Greek astronomy
    • The Metonic and Saros relations may have come from Babylonian sources. Wikipedia, Hipparchus
  2. 175 BCE–125 BCE

    Hypsicles' 360-degree zodiac and arithmetic progressions

    precursor

    Hypsicles, On Ascensions (Anaphoricus)

    Hypsicles divides the zodiac into 360 equal arcs and uses arithmetic progressions for rising times, a Babylonian-style scheme.

    Dating or attribution note

    Sources differ: Wikipedia also says Eudoxus and Timocharis already used 360 parts of the ecliptic. That the scheme is Babylonian is the usual view but was not checked in a primary study here.

    Evidence (2)
    • He was the first to divide the zodiac into 360 degrees and used arithmetic progressions. MacTutor, Hypsicles
    • The division was probably introduced for all circles by Hypsicles, in Hipparchus' time. Wikipedia, Hipparchus
  3. 147 BCE–127 BCE

    Hipparchus adopts Babylonian lunar parameters

    first statement

    Hipparchus, lost treatises on lunar motion, reported in Ptolemy, Almagest IV

    Hipparchus took Babylonian lunar period values as the basis for his Greek geometrical lunar model.

    Evidence (3)
    • For his lunar theory he needed mean motions and the best data were Babylonian parameters (Toomer quoted). MacTutor, Hipparchus
    • He seems the first to exploit Babylonian knowledge and techniques systematically. Wikipedia, Hipparchus
    • Toomer's study of the lunar epicycle size. Toomer, The size of the lunar epicycle according to Hipparchus, Centaurus 12 (1968)
  4. 147 BCE–127 BCE

    Hipparchus tests the periods against eclipse records

    first application

    Hipparchus, as described by Toomer and Ptolemy, Almagest IV.2

    He built the eclipse period of 126,007 days 1 hour and checked it on eclipse pairs, using Babylonian records in a confirming role.

    Evidence (2)
    • Toomer says the observations confirmed rather than discovered the periods. MacTutor, Hipparchus
    • Toomer suggests Ptolemy's Babylonian eclipse data came from a list made by Hipparchus. Wikipedia, Hipparchus
  5. 150

    Ptolemy's Almagest absorbs Babylonian data

    consolidation

    Ptolemy, Mathematike Syntaxis (Almagest), c. 150 AD

    The Almagest preserves Hipparchus' use of Babylonian periods and eclipse records and fixes them in the standard Greek models.

    Evidence (3)
    • Kugler showed Almagest IV.2 reports periods that Hipparchus confirmed from Babylonian ephemerides. Wikipedia, Babylonian astronomy
    • Ptolemy's knowledge of Babylonian eclipse records may come from Hipparchus. Wikipedia, Hipparchus
    • Date of the Almagest. Wikipedia, Almagest
  6. 1900

    Kugler identifies Hipparchus' Babylonian periods

    key development

    F. X. Kugler, Die babylonische Mondrechnung (Freiburg, 1900)

    Kugler found that the lunar periods Ptolemy credits to Hipparchus already appear in Babylonian System B texts.

    Evidence (2)
    • In 1900 Kugler showed that Hipparchus' periods had already been used in Babylonian ephemerides. Wikipedia, Babylonian astronomy
    • Scanned copy of the book. Internet Archive record
  7. 1975

    Neugebauer's History of Ancient Mathematical Astronomy

    consolidation

    O. Neugebauer, A History of Ancient Mathematical Astronomy (Springer, 1975)

    Neugebauer gives the standard systematic account of Babylonian astronomy and its transmission to Greek astronomy.

    Evidence (2)
    • Open Library record, 1975. Neugebauer, A history of ancient mathematical astronomy
    • Chapter on Babylonian astronomy in the same volume series. Crossref record, Babylonian Astronomy (1975)
