Mathematical physics

A curated selection of 11 recorded events in Mathematical physics. The dates describe these selected records, not the origin or size of the field.

≈ marks an approximate map placement where a dated first statement is not established.

Events in this field

  1. 1687
    Newton's Principia

    Central-force motion, universal gravitation and orbit theory were derived by geometric mathematics.

  2. 1788
    Lagrange's analytical mechanics

    Mechanics was reduced to analysis based on virtual velocities, with no diagrams.

  3. 1828
    Hamiltonian optics and mechanics

    A function from ray optics was used for dynamics.

  4. 1913
    General relativity and the absolute differential calculus

    Riemannian geometry and the tensor calculus are adopted as the language of gravitation, then fed back as a source of problems for geometry and analysis.

  5. 1918
    Noether's theorems (symmetries and conservation laws)

    Hilbert's variational derivation of gravitation led to identities that Noether tied to symmetries, giving her 1918 theorem.

  6. 1967
    Constructive quantum field theory

    Quantum fields in low dimensions are built rigorously from Markov random fields and reflection-positivity.

  7. 1967
    Twistor theory

    Penrose encodes spacetime fields as complex geometry.

  8. ≈ 1995
    Feynman periods and motives

    Particle-collision amplitudes were found to be periods of motives, and not all are multiple zeta values, prompting a search for an underlying Galois-type symmetry.

  9. 1997
    Kitaev's topological quantum computation

    Fault-tolerant quantum computation by anyons used modular tensor categories and links to Jones-type invariants.

  10. 2009
    Dynamical systems and spectral theory of Schrodinger operators

    Renormalisation and cocycle dynamics are used to describe the spectrum of quasi-periodic Schrodinger operators, solving the Ten Martini type problems.

  11. 2016
    Undecidability in quantum games via combinatorial group theory

    Systems of equations encoded as games were used to show that unlimited entanglement can win certain nonlocal games with certainty and that finite-dimensional strategies cannot.
